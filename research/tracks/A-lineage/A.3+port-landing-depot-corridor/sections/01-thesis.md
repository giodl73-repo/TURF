# Thesis

The A.3 question is whether movement infrastructure creates recognizable
current enclave terrain.

Historical ports, landings, depots, and corridor towns concentrated transfer:
people, freight, services, lodging, repair, food, civic presence, and exchange.
Modern equivalents can appear as ferry terminals, port/event districts,
airport-edge fields, rail or intermodal station areas, highway strips,
truck-stop nodes, QSR/gas/auto-service corridors, and logistics edges.

TURF should treat this as a functional analogy, not a descent claim. A field can
look port-like, depot-like, or highway-node-like because its current evidence
stack concentrates mobility and service layers. That does not prove historical
continuity, official movement boundaries, freight volume, trip purpose, or
catchment.

The reason to scaffold A.3 now is practical: A.2 has already found movement
terrain leaking into the market-town/mall story. South Philadelphia Sports /
Port, Long Beach / Lakewood, Arlington / Grand Prairie, Midway / Ford City,
Camp Creek, Perimeter, Kingston, and Bainbridge all need a movement vocabulary
that is broader than post offices and more specific than generic retail.
