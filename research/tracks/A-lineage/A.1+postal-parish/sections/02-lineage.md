# Lineage

Historical settlement systems often needed an addressable local layer: a place
where people could be registered, reached, taxed, served, gathered, or routed.
Parishes, wards, market towns, post roads, record offices, and other local
institutions performed some of those functions in different eras and countries.

TURF's postal-parish analogy is narrower than that full history. It focuses on
one function: addressability.

## Function

The modern postal/address layer helps answer:

- What is this place called for delivery and ordinary business use?
- What nearby public institution confirms that the field is part of an
  addressable service geography?
- Which public geography keys can be joined to retail rows without claiming a
  private company territory?

## Modern Translation

The modern institution is not a parish. It is a stack:

- USPS address and post-office infrastructure,
- ZIP Code and delivery-locality conventions,
- Census ZCTA approximations used for public analysis,
- public civic points such as post offices,
- commercial footprints that can be compared against those layers.

TURF treats this stack as public infrastructure and analysis context. It does
not turn postal geography into a final market boundary.

## Historical Analogy

The historical analogy is now source-gated in
`sections/07-historical-analogy-source-notes.md`.

Older parishes, market towns, record institutions, and settlement forms are
useful to TURF because they show recurring functions: local legibility,
service-center access, administrative records, and settlement identity. The
analogy stops there. TURF does not claim that USPS, ZIP Codes, ZCTAs, or
retail fields are direct descendants or equivalents of those institutions.
