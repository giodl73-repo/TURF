# Historical Analogy Source Notes

This section opens the historical-source gate for the A.1 analogy. It supports
only a functional lineage: older local institutions helped make settlement,
service, record, and market geography legible. It does not support equivalence
between those institutions and USPS, ZIP Codes, ZCTAs, or TURF fields.

## Settlement and Local Distinctiveness

Historic England's medieval settlement guidance supports a cautious premise:
settlement forms varied by region, scale, and landscape, and their study uses
documents, field survey, excavation, buildings, place names, and landscape
characterization. That is useful for TURF because it frames older settlement
geography as layered evidence, not as one clean boundary system.

Module use:

- TURF may compare modern field families to older settlement functions.
- TURF should not claim that modern retail fields preserve medieval settlement
  boundaries.
- TURF should treat differences between historical maps, landscape character,
  civic records, and retail evidence as expected rather than errors.

Reference: `historic_england_medieval_settlements`.

## Parish and Local Administration

The UK Parliament Commons Library briefing on English towns and cities
summarizes the long evolution from medieval local administration through later
county, borough, district, and parish reforms. Modern legislation also preserves
parish meetings and councils as local-government institutions in England and
Wales.

Module use:

- TURF may use "parish" as an analogy for a local administrative and civic
  legibility function.
- TURF should distinguish ecclesiastical parish, civil parish, parish council,
  municipal government, and postal/address infrastructure.
- TURF should not imply that the United States has a parish-equivalent national
  system through USPS.

References:

- `uk_parliament_local_government_towns`
- `uk_local_government_act_1972`

## Records, Poor Law, and Local Service Files

National Archives catalogue and research-guide entries show why "record office"
and "parish/vestry" are useful but narrow analogues: local institutions created
and preserved administrative records for poor relief, workhouses, vestries,
charities, paving, lighting, cleansing, watch, and related civic functions.

Module use:

- TURF may compare post offices to a modern public-facing civic/service point
  in the field stack.
- TURF may not claim that post offices perform the same functions as vestries,
  poor-law unions, or parish records.
- TURF should keep record custody, public service, addressability, and retail
  footprint as separate evidence classes.

References:

- `national_archives_workhouse_poor_law`
- `national_archives_fulham_vestry`
- `national_archives_st_anne_parish_records`

## Market Towns

Historic England area assessments for Newark-on-Trent and Boston support the
market-town analogy as a settlement and service-center function. They are
specific examples, not a universal rule.

Module use:

- TURF may describe some modern retail fields as inheriting market-center or
  service-center functions by analogy.
- TURF should not claim that malls, big-box fields, or retail corridors descend
  directly from medieval market towns without place-specific evidence.
- TURF should keep market-town analogy for later `A.2+market-town-to-mall`;
  A.1 should mention it only as a neighboring lineage.

References:

- `historic_england_newark_market_town`
- `historic_england_boston_market_town`

## Current Claim Upgrade

With these sources, A.1 can now support a narrow historical analogy:

> Older parishes, market towns, record institutions, and settlement forms show
> that local terrain has often been made legible through layered civic,
> service, administrative, and market functions. TURF uses that history as an
> analogy for interpreting modern postal/address infrastructure inside public
> retail field evidence.

The module still cannot claim:

- USPS is the modern parish,
- ZIP Codes are historical settlement boundaries,
- retail fields preserve medieval or early-modern institutions,
- historical local institutions explain present customer behavior,
- one historical lineage explains all modern enclave families.
